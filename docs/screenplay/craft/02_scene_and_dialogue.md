# Craft reference — the scene, and dialogue

**Not a skill file.** This is the full reference: read it when writing or revising
`agents/skills/screenwriter.md`, never load it into a prompt. The skill is the
distillate; this is the source.

Everything here is sourced. Where a claim could not be traced to a primary source it is
marked **[unsourced]** and must not be written into a skill as fact. Examples marked
**[constructed]** are patterns built to a principle, not quotable canon.

# PART A — THE SCENE

## A1. What a scene is

Robert McKee, *Story*, verbatim:

> "A SCENE is an action through conflict in more or less continuous time and space that
> turns the value-charged condition of a character's life on at least one value with a
> degree of perceptible significance."
> — [mckeestory.com](https://mckeestory.com/do-your-scenes-turn/)

Five load-bearing clauses, each of which can fail independently:

| clause | fails when |
|---|---|
| action **through conflict** | nobody opposes anything |
| **continuous** time and space | the "scene" is really a montage or two scenes |
| **turns** | the charge is the same at the end as the start |
| at least **one value** | you cannot name what is at stake |
| **perceptible significance** | it turns, but nobody would notice or care |

## A2. Value charge, and how to notate it

A value is a binary human quality that can flip polarity: love/hate, freedom/slavery,
truth/lie, trust/betrayal, hope/despair. McKee's diagnostic question is *"What value is
at stake in my character's life at this moment?"*

Write the value and its sign at the head and foot of the scene:

```
LOVE     (+)  →  LOVE     (--)     charge deepened negative
TRUST    (-)  →  TRUST    (+)      the scene turns positive
FREEDOM  (+)  →  FREEDOM  (+)      NONEVENT — nothing happened
```

McKee runs a deeper spectrum than simple ±:

> positive → contrary → contradictory → **negation of the negation**
> justice → unfairness → injustice → *tyranny disguised as justice*

Climaxes tend to operate at the deepest level.

## A3. The five-step scene analysis

McKee's own phrasing:

> "1 – define the conflict 2 – note opening value 3 – break the scene into beats
> 4 – note closing value, compare with opening value 5 – locate the turning point"

Expanded:

1. **Define conflict.** Who drives the scene. State the desire as an **infinitive** —
   *"to get her to admit it"*, not "wants to be happy." Name the force of antagonism.
2. **Note opening value** and its charge.
3. **Break into beats**, each labelled as an action/reaction pair.
4. **Note closing value; compare.** Identical = nonevent.
5. **Locate the turning point** — the beat where the gap opens widest and the charge flips.

## A4. Beats

A beat is *"an exchange of behavior in action/reaction"* (*Story* p. 258). Notate each
with two verbs, one per side:

```
Beat 1   asking      / ignoring
Beat 2   asking      / denying
Beat 3   waiting     / relaxing
Beat 4   insisting   / rebuking
Beat 5   accusing    / capitulating     ← TURNING POINT
OPEN: hunger (--)                        CLOSE: satisfaction (+)
```

## A5. The gap — the engine underneath all of it

> "The substance of story is a gap that splits open between what a human being expects
> to happen when he takes an action and what really does happen."

The character takes the **minimum conservative action** they believe will get what they
want. The world reacts differently, or more powerfully. The gap forces a larger, riskier
second action. The turning point is where that gap is widest.

**Corollary, and it is the most useful diagnostic in this document:** if the world
responds roughly as the character expected, the gap is not opening — and the scene has
nowhere to escalate. That is what a flat scene *is*, mechanically.

## A6. The nonevent, and why it gets written

> "If the value-charged condition of the character's life stays unchanged from one end
> of a scene to the other, nothing meaningful happens. The scene has activity — talking
> about this, doing that — but nothing changes in value. It is a nonevent."

And the cause, which is the part that matters for us:

> "The answer is almost certain to be 'exposition.' It's there to convey information
> about characters, world, or history to the eavesdropping audience. **If exposition is
> a scene's sole justification, a disciplined writer will trash it and weave its
> information into the film elsewhere.**"

This is the single most likely failure of a machine adapting a novel, because a novel
scene often exists precisely to convey information — and prose can afford that.

## A7. Text and subtext

Text = the sensory surface, the said words and visible actions.
Subtext = *"thoughts and feelings hidden by words and actions."*

> "Human nature constantly combines an outer behavior (text) with subterranean selves
> (subtext). People never say out loud exactly what they think and feel."

The warning is against scenes that **explain themselves** — that put their own subtext
into the dialogue.

## A8. Exposition as ammunition

> "Convert exposition into ammunition."

Characters know their world, their history and each other. Let them **use** what they
know as a weapon in the fight for what they want. The audience tracks the fight; the
fact goes down unnoticed. The writer must "create a motivation for the dialogue that's
greater than the facts."

## A9. Worked example — *Casablanca*, the linen-stall scene

McKee's own analysis:

- **Conflict:** Rick, to reclaim Ilsa / Ilsa, to hold the line and go on with Laszlo.
- **Opening value:** LOVE, tilted hopeful-positive — damaged by Rick's insult the night
  before, but both Rick and the audience hold a glimmer.
- **11 beats.** 1–2: Rick approaches / Ilsa ignores. 3–10: escalation, Rick baiting with
  the Paris bedroom allusion.
- **Beat 11, the turning point:** Rick implies she will betray Laszlo; Ilsa reveals she
  was *already married to Laszlo* during the Paris affair — and withholds that she
  believed him dead.
- **Closing value:** LOVE, deep negative. Hope extinguished.

The withheld clause is the whole subtext lesson: **the scene turns on what Ilsa does not
say.**

## A10. Mamet's three questions

David Mamet, memo to the writers of *The Unit*, 19 October 2005. The memo is short, in
all capitals, and its operational core is three questions to ask of every scene:

> **1) WHO WANTS WHAT?**
> **2) WHAT HAPPENS IF THEY DON'T GET IT?**
> **3) WHY NOW?**

> "DRAMA … IS THE QUEST OF THE HERO TO OVERCOME THOSE THINGS WHICH PREVENT HIM FROM
> ACHIEVING A SPECIFIC, *ACUTE* GOAL."

> "IF THE SCENE BORES YOU WHEN YOU READ IT, REST ASSURED IT *WILL* BORE THE ACTORS."

Also in the memo: avoid scenes in which characters discuss a third party; eliminate
"as you know" exposition; think visually and let the camera carry information; try
writing the scene as a silent movie.

*Sources: [No Film School](https://nofilmschool.com/2010/10/david-mamet-drama-a-memo-the-unit-writers) ·
[SlashFilm](https://www.slashfilm.com/508254/a-letter-from-david-mamet-to-the-writers-of-the-unit/) ·
[ScreenCraft PDF](https://screencraft.org/wp-content/uploads/2018/03/David-Mamet-Memo.pdf).
The full verbatim text is short enough to be worth pasting in whole — fetch it from the
ScreenCraft PDF before the skill is finalised.*

**Note the arithmetic:** three questions, not two. `WHY NOW?` is the one most often
dropped, and it is the one that kills a scene lifted straight out of a novel — a novel
can put a conversation anywhere in the chapter; a screen scene has to earn its moment.

## A11. The Five Commandments — a checkable complement

Story Grid, applied at scene scale
([storygrid.com](https://storygrid.com/five-commandments-of-storytelling/)):

1. **Inciting Incident** — "destabilizes the protagonist by upsetting the balance of
   their life for good or for ill." Causal or coincidental. Prompts a scene goal.
2. **Turning Point Progressive Complication** — attempts fail, escalating to a turn
   driven by either *character action* or *new information*.
3. **Crisis** — "poses a real choice between incompatible options with meaningful
   stakes. It is always a binary 'this or that' choice." Best Bad Choice, or
   Irreconcilable Goods.
4. **Climax** — "the active answer to the question raised by the crisis"; "always reveals
   the truth about who the AVATAR really is when they enact their choice under pressure."
5. **Resolution** — the consequence.

Worked example: *Pride and Prejudice*, Scene 19 — Collins proposes (II) → Elizabeth
refuses, he will not accept (TP) → security vs independence (Crisis) → she refuses again
(Climax) → Collins yields only when her father backs her (Resolution).

## A12. The button

The final beat or line of a scene — "the punchline of a joke, an unanswered question
that leads into the next scene, or an impactful line of dialogue." Also called a **tag**.
It "provides closure, a final touch that seals the deal," and can "add a twist, focus our
attention on a particular character or goal or theme, or emphasize an emotion."
([No Film School](https://nofilmschool.com/what-is-a-button-in-screenwriting))

Canon:

- *Casablanca* — "Major Strasser has been shot." → **"Round up the usual suspects."**
  Simultaneously plot resolution, character turn and joke.
- *Casablanca* — "Louis, I think this is the beginning of a beautiful friendship."
- *Chinatown* — **"Forget it, Jake. It's Chinatown."** Also a callback: "Chinatown" has
  been established as Gittes's private synonym for *inaction in the face of corruption*,
  so its return converts a place-name into a verdict.

# PART B — DIALOGUE

## B1. The premise

McKee, *Dialogue: The Art of Verbal Action for Page, Stage, and Screen* (2016):

> **"Talk is Cheap. Dialogue is Art."**

> "Tradition defines dialogue as talk between characters, but all talk responds to a
> need, engages a purpose, and performs an action. **To say something is to do
> something.** Therefore, beneath every line of character talk, the writer must create a
> desire, intent, and action. That action then becomes the verbal tactic we call
> dialogue."
> — [mckeestory.com/books/dialogue](https://mckeestory.com/books/dialogue/)

Scope note: for McKee "dialogue" covers character-to-character (dramatized),
character-to-self (inner voice), and character-to-reader (narratized). *"No character
ever talks to anyone, even to himself, for no reason, to do nothing."*

## B2. The three functions

**Exposition · Characterization · Action.**

- **Exposition** — "the fictional factors of setting, history, and character that readers
  and audiences need to absorb at some point so they can follow the story and involve
  themselves in its outcome."
- **Characterization** — "a character's total appearance, the sum of all surface traits
  and behaviors. It performs three functions: **to intrigue, to individualize, to
  convince**."
- **Action** — "to equip characters with the means for action. Stories contain three
  kinds of action: mental, physical, and verbal. **Mental Action:** Words and images
  compose thoughts, but a thought does not become a mental action until it causes change
  within a character — change in attitude, belief, expectation, understanding."

## B3. The three levels — the said, the unsaid, the unsayable

*Dialogue*, ch. 3, verbatim:

> **The said** — "those ideas and emotions a character chooses to express to others."
> **The unsaid** — "those thoughts and feelings a character expresses in an inner voice
> but only to himself."
> **The unsayable** — "those subconscious urges and desires a character cannot express in
> words, even to himself, because they are mute and beyond awareness."

McKee images character as **three concentric spheres**: the innermost "churning with the
unsayable," the middle "restraining the said," the outer "releasing the unsaid."

**The operational consequence, and it belongs in the skill:**
**you write the said, but you design all three.** Before writing a line, know (a) what
the character says, (b) what they are thinking and choosing not to say, (c) what they
could not name if you put a gun to their head.

## B4. On-the-nose dialogue

> "On the nose dialogue is dialogue without a subtext… The strategy to answer that
> question is to put underneath an awareness, a life underneath the dialogue."
> "The problem is in the subtext. It's not necessarily in what the characters are
> saying. It's what the characters are **doing**."
> "The dialogue becomes **tactics** characters are using in their struggle to get what
> they want."
> "The hallmark of beautiful dialogue is **transparency** — you see characters saying
> whatever they say, and you go right through those words to what they're thinking and
> feeling, even down to the subconscious level."
> — [London Screenwriters' Festival](https://londonscreenwritersfestival.com/robert-mckee-on-writing-dialogue/)

**The fix is not "be vaguer."** It is: ask the desire question, then make the line a
tactic.

> "You have to ask 'What does this character want at this point in their life?' 'What do
> they want to get in this scene as a step toward their object of desire?'"

## B5. Character-specific diction

> "**The key to character specific voices is vocabulary.** People differentiate
> themselves by the choice of words and they find their choice of words in their
> vocabulary."
> "Character determines dialogue… Ninety-nine percent of the time as you create the
> character the character then has to have a true speech style."

McKee's **Principle of Creative Limitation**: constraint breeds invention. Deliberately
forbidding a character a register forces you to find their real one.

## B6. Line design — the periodic sentence

The most immediately actionable technique in the book.

> "Excellent film dialogue tends to shape itself into the periodic sentence: *'If you
> didn't want me to do it, why'd you give me that…'* Look? Gun? Kiss?"
> "By delaying the meaning until the end of the line the audience then has to listen with
> curiosity wondering 'What is this character saying?'"

| shape | key word | effect |
|---|---|---|
| **periodic / suspense** | last | listener must stay to the end; maximum impact |
| **cumulative** | first | conversational, exploratory, spontaneous |
| **balanced** | middle | emphasis by symmetry; measured, formal |

And the performance reason it matters: *"the last word of a speech is the cue that
triggers the other actor's reaction, so misplacing the core word mid-line causes actors
to talk over each other or wait awkwardly."*

Two more, against showy writing:

> "Omit needless words… Say the absolute maximum with the fewest possible words and yet
> retain the specific nature of the characters."
> "Dialogue should not 'jump off the page.' On the other hand, it should get into the
> actor's mouth in the most natural character-specific way."

## B7. Subtext — the mechanism

Harold Pinter, "Writing for the Theatre" (Bristol, 1962):

> "There are two silences. One when no word is spoken. The other when perhaps a torrent
> of language is being employed. This speech is speaking of **a language locked beneath
> it**. That is its continual reference. The speech we hear is an indication of that
> which we don't hear. It is a necessary avoidance, a violent, sly, anguished or mocking
> smoke screen which keeps the other in its place."

Rejecting the "failure of communication" reading:

> "I think that we communicate only too well, in our silence, in what is unsaid, and that
> what takes place is a continual evasion."

Two cautions from Pinter himself, useful against imitators: he called "the weasel under
the cocktail cabinet" a remark that "meant precisely nothing," and on his own pauses —
*"if they don't make any sense, then I always say cut them."*

**Hemingway's iceberg**, *Death in the Afternoon*:

> "If a writer of prose knows enough about what he is writing about he may omit things
> that he knows and the reader, if the writer is writing truly enough, will have a
> feeling of those things as strongly as though the writer had stated them. The dignity
> of movement of an ice-berg is due to only one-eighth of it being above water."

"Hills Like White Elephants" is the executable version: the operation is never named;
the man says only *"it's an awfully simple operation… It's not really an operation at
all."* The technique is **naming the taboo object only by pronoun.**

## B8. The technique table

| technique | what it does | when it fails | before → after |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Line as tactic** | makes the line an *action* aimed at getting something | when vagueness is substituted for subtext — nobody wants anything, so there is nothing to read *through* | "You're a terrible boyfriend." / "I know, and I'm sorry, but think about the example my father set…" / "I'm breaking up with you." → "You're late." / "I called." / "I know." *She slides a box across the floor with her foot.* "The rest are by the door." **[sourced]** |
| **The oblique answer** | answers a different question than the one asked; characterizes by evasion strategy | when *every* line is oblique — the scene stops transmitting | "Where were you last night?" / **"That's so long ago, I don't remember."** *(Casablanca)* **[sourced]** |
| **The interruption** | shows one want overpowering another in real time | when used as punctuation — repeated cut-offs with no shift in who holds the floor | "I think what we should do, and I've thought about this, is—" / **"Sign it."** *(the interrupter's line must be an ACT, not just a stop)* **[constructed]** |
| **The non-sequitur** | reveals a private agenda; makes the audience supply the connection | when random rather than *motivated* — Pinter's are evasions with a target | "Did you talk to the doctor?" / "Yes. He said six months." → "Did you talk to the doctor?" / **"The hydrangeas are finished. I told you they wouldn't take."** **[constructed]** |
| **Talking past each other** | two people in the same room in different scenes; dramatizes isolation | when it reads as a continuity error — anchor it with one line of contact | "I'm frightened." / "I know. Me too." → "I'm frightened." / **"There's a nine-fifteen and a nine-forty. The nine-forty's slower but you get a seat."** **[constructed]** |
| **The "no" that means yes** | refusal in words, consent in behaviour; the gap is the characterization | when the behaviour isn't staged — on the page the reader only has the "no" | "Yes, all right, I'll come." → **"I'm not coming."** *(She is already reaching for her coat.)* **[constructed]** |
| **Silence / the refusal** | a character who will not answer has made the strongest move in the scene | when written as stage direction — `(beat)` sprayed everywhere | "Did you love him?" / "No. Not really." → "Did you love him?" / *She refills his glass.* / **"There's more in the kitchen."** **[constructed]** |
| **Pronoun for the taboo** | the unsayable stays unsaid; the reader supplies it and therefore owns it | when the referent cannot be deduced — the iceberg needs its one-eighth | *"It's an awfully simple operation… It's not really an operation at all."* **[sourced]** |
| **Escalating repetition** | the pattern itself dramatizes obsession or breakdown | when the repeated line doesn't change meaning on return | *Chinatown*: "She's my sister." / "She's my daughter." / … / **"She's my sister AND my daughter!"** **[sourced]** |
| **The callback** | a line returns in changed circumstances carrying accumulated story | when the planting was conspicuous, so the return reads as machinery — plant it as throwaway | *Chinatown*: "Chinatown" planted as Gittes's old beat → **"Forget it, Jake. It's Chinatown."** **[sourced]** |
| **The periodic sentence** | delays the key word so the listener must stay; gives the other actor a clean cue | when every line is periodic — a mannered drumbeat | "Why'd you give me that gun if you didn't want me to do it?" → **"If you didn't want me to do it, why'd you give me that… gun?"** **[sourced]** |
| **Exposition as ammunition** | delivers fact inside attack | when the "fight" is a token disagreement bolted onto a lecture | "As you know, my brother died in the fire at the mill in '78." → **"Don't you tell me about that mill. I pulled what was left of my brother out of it while you were signing the insurance."** **[constructed]** |

## B9. Overlap and dual dialogue

August and Mazin, *Scriptnotes* 470
([transcript](https://johnaugust.com/2020/scriptnotes-episode-470-dual-dialoguetranscript)):

> "It is a heavy spice, so sprinkle it with restraint." — August, on dual dialogue,
> "maybe three or four times in a script"

Mazin's two legitimate cases: two people explaining the same thing at once, and an
argument where "somebody is going to try and talk over another person" and "neither one
of them is going to stop." August's structural objection: *"we can play one voice in our
head at once. We can't play two."*

Their practical alternative is a `(overlapping)` parenthetical. And a diagnostic worth
keeping: much film dialogue that *feels* overlapping is simply **fast** — "they're just
speaking quickly… anticipating their next lines. There's just not pauses between things.
But they literally are not stacked on top of each other."

# PART C — THE PROCEDURES

## C1. Scene checklist

### HARD — fail any one and the scene is broken

| | requirement | test |
|---|---|---|
| **H1** | one value at stake, named | write `VALUE: ____`. Cannot name it in one word-pair → no scene |
| **H2** | the charge differs at the end | write `OPEN:` and `CLOSE:`. Same sign = **nonevent** |
| **H3** | a driving character wants something, as an infinitive | `WANT: to ______` — scene-sized, achievable in this room |
| **H4** | an active force of antagonism | name it. "The other character disagrees mildly" is not antagonism |
| **H5** | at least three action/reaction beats | list them as verb pairs. One beat = a moment, not a scene |
| **H6** | one identifiable turning-point beat | point to it |
| **H7** | something happens the driving character did not expect | McKee's gap. No gap, no escalation |
| **H8** | no line states the scene's own subtext | cover the dialogue — could a viewer still infer what this is about? |
| **H9** | exposition, if present, is ammunition | every fact must be *used* by the speaker to get something |
| **H10** | a deliberate last beat — a button | closure, twist, or live question. Not "and then they left" |
| **H11** | Mamet's three answer | WHO WANTS WHAT? · WHAT HAPPENS IF THEY DON'T GET IT? · **WHY NOW?** |

### SOFT — investigate, but justifiable

| | warning | why it's soft |
|---|---|---|
| S1 | opens with greetings, arrivals, settling in | usually the real scene starts later — but a ramp can build dread |
| S2 | ends after the turn with wind-down | usually cut to the turn — but a held beat can be the point |
| S3 | two characters agree throughout | alliance scenes exist, but must still turn |
| S4 | the value shifts in degree, not polarity | legitimate in a sequence's middle scenes |
| S5 | a speech runs over three sentences | break it — unless the length *is* the character |
| S6 | a character says exactly what they feel | sometimes the release of subtext IS the turn; it can't be the default |
| S7 | the information could be moved elsewhere | if yes and nothing turns, it's a nonevent |
| S8 | more than ~11 beats | McKee's *Casablanca* runs 11; far more usually means two scenes in one coat |
| S9 | silence never appears | a scene where nobody refuses to speak is often over-articulated |
| S10 | every question raised is answered | prefer leaving one live to pressure the next scene |

## C2. Voice differentiation

McKee's anchor: *"The key to character specific voices is vocabulary."* And: *"Character
determines dialogue"* — you do not decorate a voice on, you **derive** it.

0. **Derive, don't decorate.** One sentence: *what does this character believe about how
   the world works, and what are they defending?* Voice is the sound of that defence.
1. **Fix the vocabulary source.** Three wells: **occupation, formation** (education,
   class, region), **obsession**. Write 10 nouns and 10 verbs each character owns.
2. **Fix the syntax signature.** Sentence length · Anglo-Saxon vs Latinate (*get*/*obtain*)
   · do they assert or ask? · branching vs stacking · do they finish sentences or trail?
3. **Fix the register floor and ceiling.** The most formal and the coarsest thing this
   person could plausibly say. A narrow band is itself characterizing.
4. **Fix what the character CANNOT say.** Words outside their vocabulary · words their
   code forbids · the one subject they change the topic to avoid.
   **Highest-yield step**, because it generates evasions, and evasions are where subtext
   lives.
5. **Creative limitation pass.** Forbid each character one comfortable resource — one
   never uses metaphor, one never says "I", one never asks a direct question. See what
   they invent instead.
6. **The cover-the-names test.** Mask every cue and parenthetical; read only the dialogue.
   Target 100% correct attribution on distinct pairings, ≥80% within one household.
   **Every line you cannot assign is the writer's voice, not the character's.**
7. **The swap test.** Swap the two most similar characters' lines wholesale. If the scene
   still plays, they are one character in two bodies.
8. **Read aloud twice.** Once for the mouth, once listening for convergence — voices
   drift toward the writer's default under fatigue, so the second half of long scenes is
   where drift shows.
9. **Ban tics as a substitute.** A catchphrase or dialect spelling is a label, not a
   voice. At most one per cast.

*The cover-the-names test is standard workshop practice; I could not tie it to a named
originator.* **[unsourced attribution]**

## C3. Ten dialogue failure modes

| | failure | symptom | fix |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **on the nose** | characters state thoughts, feelings, intentions directly | answer the want-questions, rewrite each line as a *tactic*. Do not just make it vaguer |
| 2 | **exposition for the audience** | "As you know, Father…" | convert to ammunition |
| 3 | **the nonevent scene** | sharp talk, no change | name the value; force a flip; or cut and redistribute |
| 4 | **undifferentiated voices** | cover-the-names fails | voice procedure, esp. steps 1 and 4 |
| 5 | **tic standing in for character** | a catchphrase doing the identification work | delete the tic; if the character doesn't survive, build the voice first |
| 6 | **the speech / soapbox** | four-plus sentences, unopposed | break with counter-dialogue or action — or make the length characterizing |
| 7 | **everyone is articulate** | nobody stumbles, evades, or fails | give one character an inarticulacy that costs them the scene |
| 8 | **cliché locution** | "We need to talk." "It's not that simple." | does this line contain one word only *this* person would choose? |
| 9 | **melodrama** | emotion at maximum volume, unearned | lower the register, raise the stakes; keep words *under* the feeling |
| 10 | **flat line ending** | key word buried mid-sentence | rebuild as periodic — key word last. Vary the shape so it isn't a metronome |
