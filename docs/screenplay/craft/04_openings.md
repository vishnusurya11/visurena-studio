# Craft reference — openings

**Not a skill file.** The source. Read when writing or revising the opening-beat rules in
`agents/skills/story_editor.md` and `agents/skills/screenwriter.md`; never loaded whole
into a prompt.

`[P]` = verbatim from the primary text · `[S]` = secondary source with a URL ·
`[M]` = memory, **unverified — never publish as a quote**.

**Read §1 and §6 first.** This is the most contested area in the craft literature: the
same people who agree the opening carries disproportionate weight disagree violently about
whether there is a *list* of things it must accomplish.

---

## 1. The brick

Two sentences from Craig Mazin generate everything else in this file, and neither is a
checklist:

> "**The moment you have an opening you have limited what can come next. There's a narrow
> possibility for what comes next.** … You make a funnel. A logical funnel. But not in the
> beginning. **In the beginning there's no funnel. You can do anything.** And if you don't
> do anything interesting I don't see why people would think, well, this will get better.
> It won't." **[P — Scriptnotes 493]**

> "**The opening is our chance to be brave.** … we have two moments in movies or in any
> particular episode of television where the audience will forgive us a lot. And it's at
> the very beginning and it's at the very end. **In the middle you've got to stay in
> between the lines on the road. But in the beginning and the end you get to have fun.**"
> **[P — Scriptnotes 493]**

So: **the opening is the only position in the screenplay where the writer's freedom is
total and the audience's trust is unearned.** Both facts expire within minutes. Every rule
below is a tactic for spending one before the other runs out.

John August's statement of why the trust exists:

> "in those first minutes they really, really, really want to love what you're giving them.
> **Their guards are down.** … **they come in with a bit of trust.** And if you can sort of
> honor that trust … they're going to go on your story. **If you don't set that hook well
> they may just wander off and they may never really fully engage.**" **[P — 493]**

## 2. The one thing all sides agree on: the opening is derived from the ending

This is the strongest consensus in the entire craft literature, and it survives even the
teachers who agree on nothing else. It is also the single most actionable finding here for
a *book-adaptation* pipeline, because our pipeline knows the whole book before it writes
page one.

**Syd Field**, having just posed the question:

> "So—what's the best way to open your screenplay?
> **KNOW YOUR ENDING!**" **[P — *Screenplay*, rev. ed. 2005, p. 90]**

> "**Determine the ending of your screenplay, then design your opening.**" **[P — p. 105]**

**Blake Snyder**, in beat-sheet form:

> "the opening image has a matching beat: the final image. **These are bookends.** … **The
> opening and final images should be opposites, a plus and a minus, showing change so
> dramatic it documents the emotional upheaval that the movie represents.**" **[P — *Save
> the Cat!* ch. 4]**

> "the final image in a movie is the opposite of the opening image. **It is your proof that
> change has occurred and that it's real. If you don't have that final image, or you can't
> see how it applies, go back and check your math — there is something not adding up in Act
> Two.**" **[P — ch. 4]**

**Craig Mazin and John August**, who spend two other episodes demolishing both of the above:

> AUGUST: "**the best approach would be to figure out where your story overall wants to go
> before you write that opening scene.** Because so often you can be sort of trapped in that
> opening scene and love that opening scene but it's not actually doing the best job
> possible establishing the rest of the things you want to do."
>
> MAZIN: "100%. … *Social Network*. Opening scene, fantastic. … And at the very, very end of
> the movie he goes back to looking at that girl's profile on Facebook. She is not mentioned
> or referred to at any other time. It's just the beginning and then the end. … **The
> beginning is the end, the end is the beginning. Know them both. It will help you define
> that opening scene much, much more sharply.**" **[P — 493]**

Three paradigms, one instruction. **Treat this as settled.**

Snyder's own worked bookend, verified in the wild by a hostile critic — Suderman notes even
Pixar hits *"an opening image that's mirrored in the final scene."* **[S — Slate, 2013]**

## 3. The ten-page doctrine — and the exact line between its two halves

### 3a. The checklist version (Field)

> "You've got approximately ten pages (about ten minutes) to establish three things to your
> reader or audience: **(1) who is your main character? (2) what is the dramatic premise—
> that is, what's your story about? and (3) what is the dramatic situation—the circumstances
> surrounding your story?**" **[P — p. 90]**

> "**Ten minutes is ten pages of screenplay. I cannot emphasize enough that this first
> ten-page unit of dramatic action is the most important part of the screenplay.**"
> **[P — p. 23]**

> "**I tell my students you have to approach the first ten pages of your screenplay as a
> unit, or block, of dramatic action.** It must be designed and executed with efficiency and
> dramatic value because it sets up everything that follows." **[P — p. ~107]**

**Where the doctrine actually comes from — and it is not aesthetics, it is a slush pile:**

> "When I was head of the story department at Cinemobile, I was always seventy scripts
> behind. … I read so many screenplays that were boring and poorly written that **I learned
> to tell within the first ten pages whether a script was working or not.** … I didn't have
> time to hope the writer did his or her job; he either set up his story or he didn't. If he
> didn't, **I threw the script into the large trash bin that served as the 'return file.'**"
> **[P — p. 97]**

That provenance matters. **The ten-page rule is a description of reader behaviour that
hardened into a prescription about story design.** Knowing which one you are obeying is the
whole argument in §6.

### 3b. Field's own warning against his own rule

Worth quoting *because* it is Field, in the same book:

> "I recently read a script where **there were fifteen characters introduced within the
> first ten pages**. I didn't know who or what the story was about." **[P — p. 208]**

> "**If you've only got ten pages to grab the attention of the reader or audience, then the
> tendency is to lump all of the characters, their obstacles, and their relationships with
> other characters into this ten-page unit of dramatic action. It's too much, too soon. More
> is not necessarily better.**" **[P — pp. 208–209]**

> "**The writer tells his or her story in the first ten pages, then is lost and doesn't know
> what to do next.**" **[P — p. 208]**

**This is the failure mode our pipeline will commit by default**, because an adapter with
the whole book in context has every character available at page one.

### 3c. The weighting version — which even the critics accept

The load-bearing distinction. Mazin, who calls structural templates "synthetic," on the
same ten pages:

> "**I will spend twice as long on the first ten pages as I do on the last ten pages. The
> first ten are enormously important because they are teaching you so much.**"
> **[P — Scriptnotes 132]**

August, on his own *Big Fish*:

> "**Those first 10 pages have to do just a lot of work to sort of start the engines for
> things going.** The setup is so important…" **[P — Scriptnotes 673]**

And August's positive statement of the function, which replaces Field's checklist without
denying the weight:

> "**The first act is really how you're teaching the audience how to watch your movie.** And
> so in that initial set piece, the initial opening, you're talking about the world, you're
> talking about the characters, the tone, the voice. **You're giving them a sense of what's
> important and what's not important.**" **[P — Scriptnotes 333]**

> **Nobody disputes the ten-page weighting. The dispute is entirely about whether there is a
> list.** Encode the weighting. Do not encode the list.

## 4. Snyder's opening beats, verbatim

Reproduced because they are the most-copied opening rules in the industry and our model has
certainly absorbed a distorted version of them.

**OPENING IMAGE (1)** —

> "The very first impression of what a movie is — **its tone, its mood, the type and scope of
> the film** — are all found in the opening image. … The opening image is also an opportunity
> to give us **the starting point of the hero** … a 'before' snapshot." **[P — ch. 4]**

> "**often actors will only read the first and last 10 pages of a script** to see if that
> drastic change is in there, and see if it's intriguing." **[P — ch. 4]**

**THEME STATED (5)** —

> "**Somewhere in the first five minutes** of a well-structured screenplay, **someone (usually
> not the main character) will pose a question or make a statement (usually to the main
> character) that is the theme of the movie.** … **It won't be this obvious, it will be
> conversational, an offhand remark that the main character doesn't quite get at the
> moment** — but which will have far-reaching and meaningful impact later." **[P — ch. 4]**

> "a good screenplay is **an argument** posed by the screenwriter … the rest of the screenplay
> is the argument laid out, either proving or disproving this statement." **[P — ch. 4]**

**Correction to the common shorthand.** The phrase "**a conversation about the theme**"
does **not** appear in *Save the Cat!* (2005). It is later Save-the-Cat-brand terminology
(standard in *Save the Cat! Writes a Novel*). Snyder's words are "pose a question or make a
statement… it will be conversational." **Do not attribute the shorthand to the original.**

**SET-UP (1-10)** —

> "**this is the make-or-break section** … The set-up is also the place where **I make sure
> I've introduced or hinted at introducing every character in the A story.** … **Within the
> first 10 minutes you meet or reference them all.**" **[P — ch. 4]**

> "**the Six Things That Need Fixing.** This is my phrase, six is an arbitrary number, that
> stands for the laundry list you must show — repeat SHOW — the audience of what is missing
> in the hero's life. **Like little time bombs**, these … will be exploded later in the
> script, turned on their heads and cured." **[P — ch. 4 — closes a GAP in `01_structure.md`]**

> "There is a calm before the storm … **for things to stay as they are... is death. Things
> must change.**" **[P — ch. 4]**

**SAVE THE CAT itself**, and note Snyder's own mid-course amendment, which is almost never
quoted alongside the rule:

> "**the 'Save the Cat' scene** … It's the scene where we meet the hero and the hero does
> something — like saving a cat — **that defines who he is and makes us, the audience, like
> him.**" **[P — Introduction]**

> "**To review, Save the Cat is the screenwriting rule that says: 'The hero has to do
> something when we meet him so that we like him and want him to win.'** … So on behalf of my
> hypercritical critics, allow me a mid-course addition: **The adjunct to Save the Cat says:
> 'A screenwriter must be mindful of getting the audience "in sync" with the plight of the
> hero from the very start.'**" **[P — ch. 6]**

**The adjunct is the usable form; the cat is the mnemonic.** "In sync with the plight"
survives contact with a protagonist who is not likeable — which matters for us, because
Holmes at first meeting is deliberately off-putting.

## 5. Tactics that are not paradigm-dependent

### 5a. Mazin's punchline rule — the highest-value single rule in this file

> "**I always feel like your openings need punchlines.** … the punchline of the opening of
> the first episode of *Chernobyl* is a man hangs himself, but that's kind of the punchline
> in the sense of **there's a surprise end**. Similarly the old woman and the cow — you're
> pretty sure that soldier is going to shoot her and he doesn't shoot her. **He shoots the
> cow. Punchline.**
>
> **You need to land something surprising.** If you can, then the additional benefit you get
> from your opening is **you're putting the audience on alert that you are one step ahead of
> them** … **they are also aware that you're not just going to feed them straight up stuff,
> which is good.**" **[P — 493]**

It is decidable — *does the opening beat end on a reversal the reader did not predict?* —
which makes it checkable, unlike "make it compelling."

### 5b. The difficulty level

> "*Blade Runner* … **a challenge to you to try and keep up and a promise that it will make
> sense later.** … **It's good if the first scene gives the audience a difficulty level.** It
> doesn't have to be high difficulty … sometimes your first scene says this is going to be
> an easy play. **But let people know what the difficulty is with that first scene.**"
> **[P — Mazin, 493]**

### 5c. Don't spend it all at once — the concert rule

The anti-"start with a bang" note, and the correction to the most common bad instinct:

> "**They're hungry at the beginning. They're hungry. So don't just immediately shove all the
> food down their throat.** … When you go to a concert … it's not like the lights go down and
> then the band comes out, 'Here we are, let's go,' and then they immediately start a song.
> … they get you ready. … **So let it be happening. Don't have it just happen.**"
> **[P — Mazin, 493]**

### 5d. Disorientation as a deliberate contract

> "**more and more [openings] are about a strange kind of disorientation, a giving to you of
> a puzzle that the implied contract is this will all make sense.** … the opening sequence of
> *Breaking Bad* … was designed specifically to be **what the hell is going on. What is that?
> Why are there pants there?** … And then the puzzle gets solved." **[P — Mazin, 493]**

The word to hold onto is **contract**. Disorientation is only legitimate if the payoff is
scheduled. In our schema that means an opening marked disoriented must name the later beat
that resolves it, or it is just confusion.

### 5e. The cold open's structural privilege

The best argument in the research for why openings are categorically different, not merely
important:

> "**a cold open reveals a mystery to the audience with nobody in between.** In the normal
> method of plotting, when there are mysteries, they are discovered by and solved by and
> revealed to characters, **but not in a cold open. In a cold open, it's just you.** … Once
> we are in the perspective of our main characters, we must stay there. We can certainly see
> some things they don't see, **but we can't have scenes that are speaking directly to us.
> But you can absolutely have that at the beginning.**" **[P — Mazin, Scriptnotes 644]**

August on the test that follows from it:

> "**if you're starting in some brand new place, it would be very hard to slide this anywhere
> else in an episode.**" **[P — 644]**

> **The cold-open test: could this beat be moved anywhere else in the script? If yes, it is
> not a cold open — it is just the first scene.**

### 5f. Hard vs soft openings — genre-conditioned

Scott Myers's distinction, the cleanest available:

> "if you're writing an action or action-adventure movie, you'll most likely want to begin
> the story with a bang, a **'hard' opening sequence** that generates a high level of energy
> (as opposed to a **'soft' character-based opening**)." **[S — Go Into The Story, 2009-11-29]**

His *Sideways* reading of a soft opening is a model of how much a quiet page can carry:

> "It's a 'soft' opening, not much in the way of action, but it's funny and provides
> immediate insight into Miles' character — the hangover reference … and the line 'you didn't
> park too good' strongly suggesting that Miles has issues with alcohol. **Again we're
> curious. Again we get a key insight into the character. Plus we establish a theme.**"
> **[S — same]**

And his one hard number, which contradicts the ten-page framing usefully:

> "**Make the scene a quick one (1-2 pages).**" **[S — same]**

### 5g. Curiosity is the mechanism

> "That is an effective opening because **it creates so many questions in the mind of the
> reader, immediately creating curiosity which compels us into the story to find out the
> answers.**" **[S — Myers on *Juno*]**

Ken Miyamoto's three prohibitions, all corollaries of the same mechanism:

> "**Don't Waste Opening Pages** … don't spend the first few pages giving us exposition,
> introducing your characters, or setting up plot points.
> **Don't Assume Readers Know the Logline** … **if you spend ten pages or more building to
> something they don't know about, their interest is going to quickly wane.**
> **Don't Show Your Hand Too Early** … These details should be peppered throughout the whole
> script." **[S — ScreenCraft, via Wayback]**

The middle one is the sharpest, and it is the one an adaptation pipeline is structurally
prone to violating: **we know the logline. The reader does not.** A beat that is only
interesting once you know where the story is going is not an opening.

## 6. Where they genuinely contradict each other

| question | Field / Snyder | Mazin / August |
|---|---|---|
| Is there a list of things the opening must do? | **Yes**, and Field names three | **No.** "You don't actually have to do anything." |
| Do beats have page numbers? | Snyder: `(1)`, `(5)`, `(1-10)`, and "**I insist they do**" | "**Please do not quote me on those numbers.**" |
| Is structure something you build first? | scaffolding you hang the story on | "**Structure isn't the dog. It's the tail.** Structure is a **symptom**." |
| Are the ten pages special? | **Yes** — the make-or-break unit | **Yes** — "twice as long on the first ten pages." *No disagreement here.* |
| Know your ending before your opening? | **Yes** | **Yes.** *No disagreement here.* |

Mazin's full objection, because it is the best-argued position in the literature and it is
an argument about *direction*, not taste:

> "**All of it is done from the wrong end.** All of it. It's all done from the point of view
> of **analysis**. They look at things, they take them apart, and then they say, look, all
> these pieces fit into this swirly shape. **The issue is that's not going to help you
> actually write anything because when you write you're starting from scratch. You're not
> breaking something apart. You're building something out of nothing.**" **[P — 403]**

> "**There's never been one single great writer who created one single great screenplay
> following a structural template. Not one.**" **[P — 403]**

> "**There's a lot of what to do but where's the why?** … **If we don't know why those things
> are there how are we supposed to know how to write them?**" **[P — 403]**

His replacement for "opening image," derived rather than prescribed — and it is *better*
for us, because it is computable from a character's beliefs:

> "**Ordinary doesn't mean mundane.** … **What ordinary means here is that the protagonist's
> life essentially exemplifies their ignorance of the theme** … In fact, **they believe the
> opposite of that argument.** … **Their stasis is acceptable imperfection.**" **[P — 403]**

> "**The point of the inciting incident is not to go, 'Oh god, a meteor!' The point of the
> inciting incident is to specifically disrupt a character's stasis.** … **You don't write an
> inciting incident. … What you write is an ironic disruption of stasis.**" **[P — 403]**

August's honest defence of reading Field anyway — the lingua-franca argument:

> "**if you're going to read one book, you should probably read Syd Field, just because
> everyone else in this town has read Syd Field.** People will talk in, sort of, Syd Field
> terms whether they've read the book or not." **[P — Scriptnotes 15]**

And Mazin's actual position on Field, far more measured than his reputation:

> "**Syd Field isn't harmful, I don't think, unless you somehow view it as a religious
> choice.** … **I just think it's only harmful if people actually think that that's the book
> that's going to teach them how to be a screenwriter. It's not. There is no such thing.**"
> **[P — Scriptnotes 15]**

## 7. The strongest external evidence that the beat sheet is real — and what it costs

Peter Suderman's "Save the Movie!" is the most-cited critique, and its value here is that it
is **empirical**: it goes and finds the beats on the clock.

> "**Instead of a broad overview of how a screen story fits together, his book broke down the
> three-act structure into a detailed 'beat sheet' … and then gave each of those beats a name
> and a screenplay page number. Given that each page of a screenplay is expected to equal a
> minute of film, this makes Snyder's guide essentially a minute-to-minute movie formula.**"
> **[S — Slate, 2013-07-19]**

> "*Gangster Squad* … 'We got rules around here, smartass,' the chief growls. 'Do yourself a
> favor. Learn 'em.' **That's Snyder's second beat, theme stated. And it's right at the
> seven-minute mark, almost exactly when it's supposed to happen.**" **[S — same]**

His fair-play concession, which names the real mechanism of harm:

> "Snyder … would almost certainly dispute this characterization. … **Maybe that's what Snyder
> intended. But that's not how it turned out.** … **Intentionally or not, it's become a
> formula.**" **[S — same]**

The Field/McKee vs Snyder distinction — directly on our question:

> "**For Field and McKee, three-act structure is more of an organizing principle** … Field's
> Story Paradigm, for example, **has just a handful of general elements attached to broad
> page ranges.** … **Field and McKee offered the screenwriter's equivalent of cooking tips
> from your grandmother** — general tips and tricks to guide your process. **Snyder, on the
> other hand, offers a detailed recipe with step-by-step instructions.**" **[S — same]**

And the passage that should govern how we use *any* of this — Suderman used the beat sheet
to write the article attacking it:

> "**The formula is incredibly useful. Indeed, I relied on Snyder's beat sheet to write this
> piece, using every beat, in the order he lists.** … **But I also found myself writing to fit
> the needs of the formula rather than the good of the essay** — some sections were cut short,
> others deleted entirely, and other bits included mostly to hit the beat sheet's marks. **It
> made writing easier, in other words, but it also made me less creative.**" **[S — same]**

> **That is precisely the trade an LLM pipeline makes by default, and at a worse exchange
> rate**: a template makes a language model's output more consistent *and* more generic, and
> genericness is the failure mode we are already most exposed to. Encode the beat sheet as a
> **diagnostic** — a thing to check a plan against — never as the generator of the plan.

Suderman's demographic critique, verified against the book's own table of contents:

> "**It's not an accident that the chapter on creating a hero is called 'It's About A Guy Who
> … ' not 'It's About A Person Who … '**" **[S — same; chapter title confirmed [P]]**

## 8. What this means for a book-adaptation pipeline

**The opening is the beat you should most expect to invent.** This is the concrete
consequence of everything above, and it inverts the default instinct.

A novel's chapter one is written for a reader who has already committed — they bought the
book. It routinely opens on summary, biography, or a narrator clearing his throat.
*A Study in Scarlet* opens on Watson's military résumé: `type: nonscene`, no staged moment,
no other character present. Under §5g's rule it is disqualified outright, and under §5a it
has no punchline.

So the rules that follow for `story_editor`:

1. **The opening beat is chosen last, after the ending is fixed.** Universal consensus (§2),
   and we are in the rare position of actually knowing the ending when we start.
2. **The opening beat and the final beat are named as a pair, and the pairing is stated.**
   Snyder's bookend, Mazin's *Social Network*. If a plan cannot state the pairing, it has not
   finished.
3. **A `nonscene` may not be the opening beat.** Route it to `omitted`, or find the one
   staged moment inside it, or mark the opening `invented_connective` and say what it seeds.
4. **The opening beat must end on a reversal** — Mazin's punchline (§5a). Checkable.
5. **The opening beat may not require the logline.** Miyamoto (§5g). The single most likely
   failure for an adapter holding the whole book in context.
6. **Cap the character count in the first beat.** Field's own warning (§3b) and Snyder's
   "introduce or reference everyone in the A story" pull in opposite directions; Field's
   fifteen-character horror story is the one that matches our failure mode. **Reference is
   not introduction** — Snyder's own word is "introduced *or hinted at*."
7. **State the difficulty level** (§5b) as an explicit field of the opening beat, so the
   screenwriter downstream knows how much withholding is licensed.
8. **Do not encode page numbers.** Encode the *weighting* — the opening beat gets more
   revision passes than any other (§3c). This is the one place where the critics and the
   gurus agree, and it costs us nothing to obey both.

For `screenwriter`, one addition: **§5e's cold-open privilege is the only licence in the
whole screenplay to show the audience something no character knows.** If the plan marks a
beat as a cold open, the scene may address the audience directly; everywhere else it may
not. That is a hard constraint worth carrying, because a first-person source like Watson's
narration will tempt exactly this move in the wrong places.
