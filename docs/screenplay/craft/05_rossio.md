## 1. The brick — the strange attractor

His diagnosis after several hundred spec scripts, and it is a diagnosis about *concept*,
not craft:

> "the structure, characters, dialogue, and descriptions were all passable... and yet, in my
> heart, I knew that there was virtually no way the screenplay would ever sell."
> **[P — #02 Strange Attractor, ©1997]**

> "Put **'strange' (meaning 'unique')** and **'attractor' (from 'attractive,' meaning
> 'compelling')** together and you get 'strange attractor,' or **'something unique that is
> also compelling.'**" **[P — #02]**

And the distinction from "high concept," which is the whole point of coining a new term:

> "You could substitute 'high concept' for 'strange attractor,' but I think strange attractor
> is more precise. **What good is a short, simple idea for a movie if it doesn't also attract
> people?**" **[P — #02]**

His own demonstration — the same premise without, then with, an attractor:

> "A man wrongly convicted of murder runs his investigation from the confines of his jail
> cell."
> → "A man wrongly convicted of murder **learns to astral-project himself out of his jail
> cell**; he must locate the real killer in order to clear his name."
> "Still, as hokey as this sounds, **at least it has an identifiable strange attractor.**"
> **[P — #02]**

The quality bar and the test:

> "There is a certain **'aha'** that comes with these last ideas. You get the feeling that
> exploring them will lead to interesting situations, and compelling drama." **[P — #02]**

> "The best ones explore a bit of the human condition that is **specific, universal, and (if
> possible) has never been done before.**" **[P — #02]**

> "**You need to know exactly what it is. You should be able to point to it and talk about
> it, the same way you talk about characters and theme and plot.**" **[P — #02]**

## 2. #27 Adaptive Behavior — the only adaptation column by a working screenwriter in this corpus

**The most directly applicable column in the whole set.** Rossio ranks nine elements by
whether to preserve or change them: GENRE · TONE · THEME · CHARACTERIZATIONS · DIALOG
RHYTHM · BASIC SITUATION · KEY ELEMENTS · PLOT & STORY · THE SETTING.

The governing statement, which should be the first line the adapter reads:

> "**Your goal in writing an adaptation absolutely cannot be to 'preserve the source material
> onto the screen.' It must be to 'make an effective film based upon the source
> material.'**" **[P — #27 Adaptive Behavior]**

Lorenzo DiBonaventura, quoted by Rossio:

> "**Sometimes keeping too true to the material results in not doing justice to the
> material.**" **[P — #27]**

**And the ranking itself — this is the part that is genuinely actionable, because it tells
you what to spend the budget protecting:**

> "It might not seem so at first, but **plot is the least important element to retain from a
> property. The events which occur must remain malleable.** … Yet scenes which are changed,
> and new scenes invented, **must continue to feel as though they live in the world of the
> original property.**" **[P — #27]**

> "**Identify the basic situation. Keep the basic situation.**" **[P — #27]**

> "**Characters are often the key element in any adaptation. If you can maintain the essence
> of the characterizations and character relationships, you've won half the battle.**"
> **[P — #27]**

> "**The essential elements that made something good in one medium rarely survive both a
> change of medium and a change of genre.**" **[P — #27]**

The success criterion, and it is a *reader-experience* criterion, not a fidelity one:

> "**the true measure of success is whether you're able to duplicate in the film medium the
> experience the audience felt with the property in its original form.**" **[P — #27]**

> **This resolves a tension left open in `story_editor.md`.** That skill says "prefer merging
> to cutting" and "order is preserved," which reads as a fidelity bias. Rossio supplies the
> priority order those rules were missing: **situation and character are protected; plot is
> the first thing spent.** The monotonic-order finding in `01_structure.md` is an empirical
> observation about what adapters *did*, not a licence to preserve plot at the cost of
> situation.

## 3. #30 The Task — goal vs task

His correction to "protagonist needs a goal, put obstacles in his way," which he calls
"only about half right."

> "**The GOAL** in a film is often a positive event, tied to the essential nature of the
> protagonist... **The TASK** is often the opposite — **an external problem, imposed by the
> antagonist, fundamentally at odds with the basic nature of the protagonist.**" **[P — #30]**

> "**Unique and particular instead of universal, most often distasteful, the task is almost
> never something the audience would choose to experience directly.**" **[P — #30]**

Why the task is where the film's identity lives — the best single line in the column:

> "**There are many films with the goal 'to find your way home.' But there's only one film
> where a girl clicks together a pair of ruby slippers.**" **[P — #30]**

And the task generates the characterization, not the other way round:

> *Silence of the Lambs* — noble goal, hideous task. "So, what's the characterization designed
> to fully exploit the situation? **A young, inexperienced, idealistic, attractive, female
> Federal Investigator.**" **[P — #30]**

**The default tasks — his list of symptoms that you skipped this work:** RUN ACROSS TOWN ·
RESCUE THE GIRL · KILL THE VILLAIN · DE-FUSE THE BOMB · and worst:

> "the hero must **COME TO A DECISION** at the end of Act II or in the middle of Act III.
> **It's usually just not very filmic to watch somebody change their minds...** (And when you
> see this choice, **it's almost guaranteed to take place in a script that has a 'passive
> lead' problem**.)" **[P — #30]**

The test, which is a *silent-film* test and therefore mechanically checkable against a shot
list:

> "In its best form, the resolution of the task, as a physical action, becomes a powerful
> filmic image for the movie. **You could literally turn the sound down, and graphically see
> the story play itself out to its completion.**" **[P — #30]**

> "**You'd better invent a task for your screenplay. Or your screenplay will invent a task
> for you.**" **[P — #30]**

## 4. #49 Situation-Based Writing — the scene-level law

One claim, argued for two thousand words: the screenwriter's job is **interest**, and
situation is its strongest generator.

> "**Okay, every scene you write needs to be a clear and understandable situation. There it
> is. Every single scene. A clear and understandable situation. No exceptions.**"
> **[P — #49, ©2008]**

> "**The first and foremost task your screenplay needs to accomplish is... create interest.
> That's the coin of the realm.**" **[P — #49]**

The exposition claim, which is more radical than anything in `02_scene_and_dialogue.md` and
worth holding as the extreme position:

> "**Here's the stunning truth about exposition. You don't need it.** … **The audience does
> not need to know. The audience needs to wonder.**" **[P — #49]**

> "One of the hallmarks of good situation-based writing is **the audience is permitted to
> speculate ahead, to future events.**" **[P — #49]**

> "**You don't really even need to worry about plot.** Why think in those terms? You'll fall
> into the exposition trap. **What you need are a series of events, events that are
> situations. String together a series of those, and guaranteed you will have a plot.**"
> **[P — #49]**

His inversion of "write interesting characters," which is a genuinely different causal claim:

> "**we don't watch interesting characters who go around wanting things... we see characters
> who want things and find them to be interesting.**" **[P — #49]**

**`shoe leather`** is his term and it is the one to adopt: *"the term for all scenes in a
screenplay that are not situations."* Alongside it: **immediacy**, **imperative**,
**consequences**. Situations arise from exactly two sources — **circumstance** (pushed in
from the world) and **strong character want** (pushed out from inside).

## 5. #40 The Off-Screen Movie — where momentum actually comes from

The insight arrived at watching the hinge scene in *Schindler's List*:

> "**it's the off-screen movie, the scenes of the story you don't see, that create momentum
> in the scenes you do see.**" **[P — #40, ©1999]**

Ted Elliott's line, which Rossio treats as the rule:

> "**So the way to make a boring movie is to show everything.**" **[P — #40]**

> "**Most scenes need to be at least somewhat incomplete in order to propel the story**; one
> fights the impulse to make each scene individually satisfying, rather than let scenes
> service the overall story." **[P — #40]**

> "**In order to have surprises, twists and reveals in your story, you must hide stuff
> off-screen for a period of time.**" **[P — #40]**

> "what you tell an audience can create drama, sure... but **it's what you don't tell 'em that
> really drives 'em nuts.**" **[P — #40]**

**This is the missing justification for "enter late, leave early"** (`01_structure.md` §9).
That rule is usually taught as a pacing trick. Rossio's version explains *why* it works and
therefore when it doesn't: the cut buys momentum only if something is actually happening in
the gap. Cutting into a scene late when nothing happened off-screen just makes the scene
shorter.

## 6. #41 Point of View — a constraint engine, and the one we most need

Directly relevant, because our source is Watson's first-person narration and our schema
already tracks who witnesses what.

> "**Correctly employed, POV does nothing less than tell you which scenes you're allowed to
> write, and which scenes you're not allowed to write.**" **[P — #41]**

> "**A general rule: the more limited the point of view, the more elegant, and effective,
> your story.**" **[P — #41]**

> "**the omniscient point of view, the one that goes anywhere and shows anyone, anytime, is
> perhaps the least interesting choice for a film. It's pretty much a non-choice.** … **no
> limits can give the impression of no form.**" **[P — #41]**

> "One reason: **when a film is about everything, it's hard for it to be about anything.**"
> **[P — #41]**

His named POV limitations: **SINGLE PROTAGONIST · THE ROMANCE · BITTER RIVALS · THE ENSEMBLE
PIECE**, with the metaphor of a camera strapped to a parrot for limited third person.

And the observation that should make an adapter re-examine what the main relationship is:

> "It might be a surprise to note that **the main relationship of RAIDERS is between Indy and
> Belloq, not Indy and Marion.**" **[P — #41]**

> **For us this is a hard rule, not a suggestion.** A Watson-narrated source has a built-in
> POV limit that the novel obeys and an adapter will casually break the moment it becomes
> convenient — a scene at the murderer's lodgings with no Watson present is a scene the
> source's own form forbids. Breaking it is a legitimate adaptation choice; **breaking it
> without noticing is the failure.** Pair this with `04_openings.md` §5e: the cold open is the
> one licensed exception.

## 7. #14 Anthropic Principle — killing coincidence

The rule most useful to a detective adaptation, where the plot runs on convenient discovery:

> "One of the classic rules of coincidence is that **fate — if it must be present — should
> always favor the antagonist.**" **[P — #14]**

Walter Parkes's refinement:

> "**If the hero must — due to the needs of the story — catch a break, at least don't let it
> happen at the best possible moment. Make it happen at the worst possible moment.**"
> **[P — #14, quoting Parkes]**

And the technique that actually dissolves a coincidence rather than excusing it:

> "**When the coincidental event springs from the same action that created the need for the
> coincidence, the coincidence is killed.**" **[P — #14]**

His initial-conditions model, which is a story-engine test we can run against a scene
dossier: set each character's wants and possessions at page one, add Time, then —

> "**The mark of a bad screenplay is when the initial conditions do not change, change very
> little, change in unsatisfying ways, or change in such a way so as to hit a dead-end.**"
> **[P — #14]**

## 8. #20 Story Molecule — a ready-made scoring rubric

The only explicit scoring list in this corpus, and it is directly implementable as a
critique rubric for a beat or a scene.

> "the first-and-only rule for all story molecules is: **bigger is better.** That's the only
> criterion on how we're going to judge them. **Choose the molecule that's bigger.**"
> **[P — #20]**

| BIGGER | SMALLER |
|---|---|
| visual · emotional · filmic · unique · funny · effective · on theme · dramatic · smart · concise · interesting · organic · appropriate | cliché · dumb · ineffective · contradictory · boring · long · overly complex · **internal** · unbelievable · coincidental · mean-spirited · annoying · off theme · disruptive of tone |

**[P — #20, both lists verbatim]**

Note `internal` sitting in the SMALLER column — it is the same finding as the four-elements
rule in `screenwriter.md`, arrived at from a completely different direction.

## 9. #51 Scene Character — the rendering step most writers skip

He writes a flat "assassin plants a bomb at a suburban home" scene, then rewrites it with an
ice-cream man, lawn gnomes, and a little girl who notices "Mister Muggins moved!"

> "**You need to characterize your scenes, too.**" **[P — #51, ©2008]**

His verdict on the flat version is the sharpest quality-control sentence in the corpus:

> "**It's not whether you did a good job or you did a bad job. I maintain you didn't do the
> job at all. There's no way to even judge the scene, because it's null.** There is no voice,
> no tone, no spin, no atmosphere, no... anything. … **There's nothing there that they'd have
> to pay someone to write, it's a scene anyone could do.**" **[P — #51]**

> "**What you do is ask the question, what sort of character could the scene have that would
> best tell the story?**" **[P — #51]**

> "**Some scenes in a screenplay play best as seduction scenes, even if there isn't an obvious
> seduction going on. Some scenes are about power, or control, or dominance, even if those
> words are never spoken.**" **[P — #51]**

> "**And really, until you know the character of a scene, how do you even write it? What
> touchstone do you have to navigate all those choices?**" **[P — #51]**

> **"A scene anyone could do" is the exact failure mode of an LLM writing from a scene
> dossier**, and this column names it and supplies the fix as a single answerable question.
> That makes it a candidate field on the scene contract, not just advice.

## 10. #13 The Big Finish — the ending rule

> "**Here's my iron-clad rule for how a movie should end.** … **A good ending must be
> decisive, set-up, and inevitable — but nonetheless unexpected.**" **[P — #13]**

> "**You want an ending that is so 'right,' it seems as if it could have turned out no other
> way — but only after it's happened!**" **[P — #13]**

> "I think of endings as **the fulfillment of the promise, the covenant the storyteller makes
> with the audience.** Violate that covenant at your peril." **[P — #13]**

The operational technique, which is a question-type taxonomy:

> "**I find it useful to consider which type of question or questions is truly unknown to the
> audience.** … **So if you know which answer you're holding back — the WHO, WHY, WHAT, HOW,
> or WHERE — it can be easier to create that all-important unexpected ending. The best to go
> for is the WHAT question.**" **[P — #13]**

> "Note that **'spectacle' is not one of the requirements for an ending. You don't necessarily
> need a big finish — what you need is a satisfying conclusion to the situation.**" **[P — #13]**

**Pair this with `04_openings.md` §2.** Every school agrees the opening derives from the
ending; Rossio is the one who says what the ending must *be* in terms concrete enough to
check — decisive, set up, inevitable, unexpected — and for a mystery adaptation the
WHO/WHY/WHAT/HOW/WHERE taxonomy is exactly the right instrument, because the source has
already chosen one and the adaptation can choose differently.

## 11. #32 Plot Devices — the named techniques worth keeping

A grab-bag column, each item of which was once slated to be its own column. The ones that
earn their place here:

**Widows and orphans** — the cleanest available names for a failure our `story_editor` skill
already checks for without naming:

> "**Widows are setups that have no payoffs, because the payoffs have been cut. Orphans are
> payoffs that have no setups.**" **[P — #32, ©1998, with Ted Elliott]**

**Externalize the story:**

> "While it's great for characters to have strong inner lives, **it's a mistake to organize a
> plot around the drama of a character deciding to change their thinking.** … it's the task of
> the screenwriter to **take that inner journey of the character and give it a playing
> area.**" **[P — #32]**

**Make the plot moment a character moment** — a story-hole repair technique:

> "**Move the weak scene away from the logic-driven arena of plot, and into the
> emotion-driven arena of character, and you won't believe the story holes you can hide.**"
> **[P — #32]**

**Story vs. plot** — a definition worth adopting because it is *directional*:

> "**The plot of your screenplay is what happens. The story is the particular way you choose
> to reveal to the audience what happens.** … **Very rarely is telling the plot the most
> effective way to tell the story.**" **[P — #32]**

**Go to the extreme:**

> "**Characters, stories, and story beats fail far more often by not going far enough than by
> going too far.** It is almost a rule that **if you push a character, an emotion, or a
> situation to the absolute extreme, it will play on film.**" **[P — #32]**

**Smart villains, smarter heroes** — and note who he blames:

> "It's a great temptation to fix a problem not by coming up with a more clever solution on
> the part of the hero, but **by making the villain dumb, so that the hero looks good by
> comparison. (This is a favorite technique among development executives.) Don't do it.**"
> **[P — #32]**

**The hero taxonomy**, which is more useful than "protagonist" alone:

> "**The DRAMATIC HERO succeeds due to his best efforts. The TRAGIC HERO fails despite his
> best efforts. And the COMIC HERO succeeds despite his best efforts** — usually to avoid
> heroism." **[P — #32]**

**60 two-minute scenes** — Lucas via Rossio, and it is the same arithmetic Myers reaches by
another route (`01_structure.md` §11):

> "George Lucas once said that a film is **'Sixty great two-minute scenes.'** … Keep this
> structure in your head as you plot, and the content of your screenplay will naturally focus
> on the true, necessary beats of your story." **[P — #32]**

Also in the column, unquoted here: THE WANG THEORY · THE MacGUFFIN · PLAY THE BEATS · THE
CLONE WARS · THREE GREAT SCENES NO BAD ONES · DON'T CUT THE LAST THING FOR A CHARACTER ·
SUBTEXT/SUBPLOT · AUDIENCE SUPERIOR/INFERIOR · REVERSALS · THE RICK · IF IT'S NOT GOOD AT
LEAST MAKE IT SHORT · THE GILLIGAN CUT · THE MAIN RELATIONSHIP THEORY · KIDNAPPING.

## 12. #48 Dramatic Irony — his most ambitious craft claim

> "**Dramatic irony is a joke that's not funny — But not because it's a bad joke.**"
> **[P — #48, ©2007]**

> "Dramatic irony has **exactly the same qualities in construction as a joke, but occurs too
> slowly to be funny**; instead the relationship between what happens in the context of what
> is known invokes a feeling of **delight, satisfaction, or understanding, rather than
> laughter.**" **[P — #48]**

> "**The presence of dramatic irony might not guarantee you a masterful screenplay, but the
> lack of it may very well mean your screenplay can never be masterful.**" **[P — #48]**

Two constraints that make it operational rather than mystical:

> "**It almost always makes use of the 'audience superior' position.**" and irony "**can only
> be achieved relative to story elements the dramatist has emphasized, or that pre-exist in
> the audience.**" **[P — #48]**

The second clause is the reason a *known* source text is an asset: with Sherlock Holmes,
enormous amounts pre-exist in the audience, which means irony is available at a discount that
an original screenplay does not get.

## 13. #03 Beachcombing — the concept-attribute checklist

The operational companion to #02. His attributes of salable concepts (his lettering skips G):
**A. Larger World Revealed · B. Universal-ness · C. Classic Echoes · D. Implies a Situation ·
E. Behind the Scenes · F. Good Roles (and Good Titles) · H. Concept Cannot Be Done Again ·
I. Known Elements.**

> "Thematically, **often the best film concepts show the world to be a larger, more magical,
> more complex place than is commonly believed.** Or the story reveals how much larger, more
> magical and more complex the human spirit is than is commonly believed." **[P — #03]**

> "**In many cases the concept is a situation... and in resolving that situation, you get your
> sequences for Acts II and III.**" **[P — #03]**

> "Science fiction writer Harlan Ellison pointed out that one of the defining attributes of a
> good 'high concept' was that **once it was done, the idea was fully defined and explored,
> and nobody could do it again.**" **[P — #03]**

> "**Good film concepts tend to utilize elements that already exist within the awareness of
> the audience.**" **[P — #03]**

And the line that should govern how long we let a plan stay in revision:

> "**Most spec scripts which end on page 110 actually should end on page 35 — because that's
> all the story that's really there; it's just been dragged out to fill the page
> requirement.**" **[P — #03]**

## 14. Two more worth having

**#08 Impressive Failure** — how a hero should lose, which matters because a mystery's middle
is a sequence of failures:

> "**In plotting an action-adventure type film, how your hero fails is at least as important
> as how he succeeds. If the plot requires the hero to fail, try to figure out a way for him
> to fail as impressively as possible.**" **[P — #08]**

> "Indiana Jones … **in his debut film, he flat-out fails from beginning to end.** … Because
> he fails so damn impressively, from start to finish. **Indy fails so well, in fact, the
> audience is impressed as hell, and hardly aware of the fact that he's failing. The defeats
> are just setbacks that create more opportunities for heroism.**" **[P — #08]**

The counter-example is the diagnostic: *Waterworld*'s Mariner loses boat and child by
wandering off — "**The audience actually loses sympathy for the hero.**"

**#29 Deep Thoughts** — theme, and two techniques for stating it without a lecture:

> "**Theme is not an element added. It is the well from which all other elements spring.** …
> plot, story, character, tone... **theme in motion sets them all, theme defines them all...
> and theme unifies them all.**" **[P — #29]**

> "One technique is to **shape the villain's character to illustrate the dark side of whatever
> positive theme the protagonist's journey reveals.**" / "One trick Ted and I use is to **let
> the villain articulate the theme.**" **[P — #29]**

> Zemeckis/Gale: "**let the sub-plot state the theme**" (*Back to the Future*) — "**because it
> was couched in sub-plot, it didn't land as too heavy-handed.**" **[P — #29]**

**The "one step further" test**, which is a concrete anti-cliché procedure: pitch your setup
to a stranger; whatever cliché theme they name ("Love conquers all"),

> "**push the 'cliché' theme one step further. Do a story about the fact that sometimes love
> doesn't conquer all — and how do you live with that?**" **[P — #29]**

And an aphorism worth keeping for scope decisions:

> "**You can have a small idea for a small movie, or a big idea for a big movie, or even a big
> idea for a small movie, but you can't have a small idea for a big movie.**" **[P — #29]**

the whole reason for this file. The terms worth carrying into skill files and schemas:
