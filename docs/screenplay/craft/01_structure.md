# Craft reference — structure

**Not a skill file.** The source. Read when writing or revising
`agents/skills/story_editor.md`; never loaded into a prompt.

`[P]` = verbatim from the primary text · `[S]` = secondary source with a URL ·
`[M]` = memory, **unverified — never publish as a quote**.

**Read §5 (the consensus) and §6 (the contradictions) before anything else.** The single
biggest risk in this domain is asserting a contested rule as fact, because the teachers
disagree loudly and a language model will reproduce whichever one it saw most.

## 1. The consensus core — what nearly everyone agrees on

Cross-checked against Aristotle, Freytag, Field, Snyder, McKee, Truby, Vogler, Yorke,
Gulino, Harmon, Mazin, August, Rossio. Dissent noted where it exists.

| | rule | dissent |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Open on a stable but flawed state, and make the flaw visible.** | none |
| 2 | **Disrupt it early with an event tailored to *this* character.** | only on the page number |
| 3 | **Force an irreversible commitment at roughly one quarter in.** | none |
| 4 | **An external, specific, visible goal — plus an internal thing they don't know they need.** | Mazin drops the want/need binary but keeps the substance |
| 5 | **Give the opposition the SAME goal, not a different one.** | Truby goes further — minimum four corners |
| 6 | **Escalate: each obstacle costs more, each easy solution is eliminated.** | none |
| 7 | **Put a reversal at the centre that changes what the protagonist KNOWS.** | they disagree on *direction*, not existence |
| 8 | **Strip the protagonist of their old strategy before the climax.** | Gulino denies it must be a *low point* |
| 9 | **The climax is an ACTION that PROVES the change — never a statement of it.** | **none whatsoever** |
| 10 | **Close on a state mirroring the opening; cut anything that doesn't turn.** | none |

**Rule 9 is the strongest agreement in the entire literature.** Truby: *"The moral
decision is the proof of what the hero has learned."* Mazin: *"It's not enough to say,
OK, I get it… **They have to prove it**… in a way where they literally embody the point
of that idea with everything they have."* Snyder: the hero "proves they've learned the
lesson." Aristotle: *"in the form of action, not of narrative."*

An eleventh, near-consensus with one objector: **structure is fractal.** Freytag 1863,
Gulino 2004, Yorke 2013, Harmon. Mazin objects to the framing.

## 2. Mazin — structure as symptom

Scriptnotes **Ep. 403**, "How to Write a Movie." *(Correction to an earlier note of mine:
**Ep. 405 is not part two.** There is no part two. 405 is a comedy panel.)*

> "**Structure I'm here to tell you is a total trap.** … Structure doesn't say this
> happens on this page… Here's a pinch point. Here's a stretchy point. … **If you follow
> strict structural guidelines in all likelihood you will write a very well structured
> bad script.**"

> "**Structure isn't the dog. It's the tail. Structure is a symptom. It's a symptom of a
> character's relationship with a central dramatic argument.** … **Structure is not a
> tool, it is a symptom.**"

> "**There are no acts.** So you can't really be scared of the second act. It doesn't
> exist… **There's one act. It's called your story.**"

### The central dramatic argument

> "**the argument has to be an argument.** … they think a theme could be *brotherhood*.
> Well, no. **Because there's nothing to argue about there.** … But *man and women can't
> just be friends*, well, that's an argument. ***Better to be dead than a slave.*** *Life
> is beautiful, even in the midst of horrors.* **Those are arguments.**"

> "**You probably don't want to start with an argument.** … when you think of the idea the
> very next question you should ask is **what central dramatic argument would fit really
> well with this? And ideally you're going to think ironically.**"

*Finding Nemo*, his own demonstration: pair *a fish needs to find another fish* with
***"No matter how much you want to hold onto the person you love, sometimes you have to
set them free."*** — "**because when you're looking for somebody out there in the deep,
deep ocean you the writer know that what you're promising is they're going to find them
and then have to let them go anyway.**"

**Precision:** Mazin does **not** state an "It is better to X than Y" fill-in-the-blank.
His test is *an arguable proposition a reasonable person could deny.* Don't attribute a
template to him. **[S]**

### The purpose, and the test

> "**The purpose of the story is to take a character from ignorance of the truth of the
> theme to embodiment of the theme through action.**"

> "**If you remove everything from the story except the introduction of your hero and the
> last scene of your hero there should really be only one fundamental difference… The
> hero in the beginning acts in accordance with the anti-theme and the hero at the end
> acts in accordance with the theme.**"

### The rest of the engine

- **Stasis:** "their stasis is **acceptable imperfection**." The inciting incident's job
  "is not to go, 'Oh god, a meteor!' … **it is to specifically disrupt a character's
  stasis**" — "**ironic as in a situation… genetically engineered to break your
  character's soul.**"
- **Heroes as cowards:** "**Your heroes should be on some level cowards.** … *I don't
  want to change. I'm happy with the way things are.* … **And fear, especially in your
  character, is the heart of empathy.**" What do they want? "**They want to go
  backwards.**"
- **Escalation by irony** (*Nemo*): no mom → "**your wife and all of your other children
  were eaten in front of you**" → "**and that is still not enough**" → the surviving kid
  has a bad fin. "**Don't just stab your characters. Twist the knife.** … **You are the
  Old Testament god who tortures Job.**"
- **The low point, re-derived:** "**It's not the low point because the books say page 90
  is the low point. It's the low point because your character is lost.** … They're
  trapped between rejection of the old and acceptance of the new."
- **The relapse** — his most distinctive prescription: right before the decision, "**hold
  that safety blanket up and say, 'Go ahead. Go back to the beginning… Don't change. Go
  back.'** And what do they have to do? **They have to reject that temptation.**"
- **Passive protagonists:** "**They have to make the choices or you're making it for them.
  And then, well, it just doesn't count, does it?**"

## 3. The sequence — Gulino

*Screenwriting: The Sequence Approach* (2004), full text. **This is the most useful
structural idea for us**, because a sequence is the unit our 92 analysis scenes should
group into.

> "A typical two-hour film is composed of sequences — **eight- to fifteen-minute segments
> that have their own internal structure — in effect, shorter films built inside the
> larger film.** … **the conflicts and issues raised in a sequence are only partially
> resolved within the sequence.**"

> "In general, a two-hour film will have **two fifteen-minute sequences in the first act,
> four in the second, and two more in the third.**"

### The fractal claim — the cleanest citable sentence in the literature

> "Any time there is dramatic tension, there are three parts: it must be set up, played
> out, and resolved. **In film, the 'three act structure' is something like a fractal in
> geometry: a nested structure, iterated at three different levels.**"

*Double Indemnity*: **act** — will he succeed? **sequence** — can they set up the murder
undiscovered? **scene** — can they get Dietrichson to sign?

### The eight sequences

**⚠️ Correction to my earlier draft.** Gulino labels them only **A–H**. The catchy names —
"lock-in," "raising stakes," "subplot" — are **popularizer vocabulary** (Final Draft blog,
Sokoloff), *not his*, and they smuggle in an Act-II low point he explicitly rejects.

| | function |
|---|---|
| **A** | Hook by **curiosity** first, exposition second. Establish the **flow of life** — *"the stronger the sense of flow of life at the beginning, the bigger the impact of the destabilizing events."* Ends at the **point of attack**. |
| **B** | **Sets up the main tension.** Ends act one (~25%). Attempts lead to a bigger **predicament**. |
| **C** | **First attempt** — "characters tend to choose the easiest solution." |
| **D** | Desperate measures → **First Culmination / midpoint**: "a very clear glimpse of an answer… **only to see circumstances turn the story the other way**" — "the actual resolution of the picture, **or its mirror opposite**." |
| **E** | The new complication; subplots; "**stakes that are higher still**." |
| **F** | "**having eliminated all the easy potential solutions**" — the dramatic question is answered. **Second Culmination**, end of act two. |
| **G** | Unexpected consequences, "**still higher stakes and a more frenzied pace**," a **major twist**. |
| **H** | Resolution + **epilogue or coda** — "a chance to catch its breath." |

**Gulino explicitly rejects the "low point" convention:**

> "It is a common misconception that the end of the second act needs to be a 'low point'
> … only three films in this volume … could be said to follow this pattern. **Seeing it as
> a 'low point' cuts off a writer from a great many story possibilities.**"

And: *"the main tension is **not** resolved at the end of the picture; in most cases it is
resolved at the end of the second act"* — *North by Northwest* 77%, *Saving Private Ryan*
70%, *Nights of Cabiria* 82%.

### The generative question — the most useful line in the book

> "Instead of asking 'how do I make the pursuit of this woman fill up sixty or ninety
> minutes?' it is much more fruitful to ask **'what is the quickest and easiest way for
> this character to get the girl?'** … **a writer needs to have the courage to let the
> second act end after only fifteen pages**, if the protagonist can achieve his objective
> in that time. It is then easy enough to brainstorm developments that foil this outcome."

### The Big Four

1. **Telegraphing** — the **appointment**, the **deadline/ticking clock**, and **false
   telegraphing** (paid off in reverse = the twist). *"A character getting kidnapped in
   the opening shot can never be a surprise twist, because no expectation has yet been
   created."*
2. **Dangling cause** — "an expression of intent, a warning, a threat, an expression of
   hope or fear, or a prediction, which places a question in the audience's mind for
   which no immediate answer is provided." Sub-form: the **dialogue hook**.
3. **Dramatic irony** — bracketed by **revelation** (audience learns) and **recognition**
   (character learns); the gap between is **ironic tension**. Flavours: suspense, comic.
4. **Dramatic tension** — the dramatic question → the **main tension**.

### Frank Daniel

> **"Somebody wants something badly and is having difficulty getting it."**

Two story types: **chases and escapes.** Daniel revived sequences because "**teaching the
three-act approach resulted in the difficulties discussed above**" — the 60-page second-act
swamp.

**"24 sequences" and "the central question" appear nowhere in Gulino.** Both are likely
conflations. **[do not use]**

### The per-sequence schema — the most machine-implementable object in the literature

Gulino annotates every sequence in his charts with:
**Unifying Aspect · Protagonist · Objective · boundary event.**
That is a schema, not a vibe. Use it.

## 4. The rest of the canon, compressed

### Truby — *The Anatomy of Story* [P, full text]

**Seven key steps:** weakness and need · desire · opponent · plan · battle ·
self-revelation · new equilibrium. *"These seven steps are the nucleus, the DNA."*
**22 steps** exist but *"a story may have more or fewer… **It must have no fewer than the
seven**."*

- **Need vs desire:** "Need has to do with overcoming a weakness *within*… Desire is a
  goal *outside*. **One of the biggest mistakes a writer can make is to confuse need and
  desire.**"
- **Never open with desire:** "They think, 'I'll just skip the weakness-and-need step.'
  **They've just made a pact with the devil.** … Don't skip that first step. Ever."
- **The opponent rule, and it is the sharpest in the book:** "A true opponent … is
  **competing with the hero for the same goal**. If you give your hero and opponent two
  separate goals, each one can get what he wants without coming into direct conflict.
  **And then you have no story at all.**"
- **Four-corner opposition:** "**A simplistic opposition between two characters kills any
  chance at depth.**… hero plus **at least two secondary opponents**… each attacks the
  hero's great weakness **in a different way**."
- **Moral vs psychological need:** "A psychological need involves overcoming a serious
  flaw that is hurting **nobody but the hero**. … **A character with a moral need is
  always hurting others.**"
- **The designing principle:** "**Designing principle = story process + original
  execution**." *Tootsie* — "**Force a male chauvinist to live as a woman.**"
  *"Most stories don't have one… That's the difference between a premise, which all
  stories have, and a designing principle — which only good stories have."*
- **The body analogy:** "**theme is the brain… Character is the heart… Revelations are the
  nervous system. Story structure is the skeleton. Scenes are the skin.**"

**Against three acts:** "*hopelessly simplistic and in many ways just plain wrong* …
**act breaks are external to the story** … **A mechanical view of story, like three-act
theory, inevitably leads to episodic storytelling.** … The average hit film today has
**seven to ten major reveals.**"

### Field — and the passage nobody quotes [P]

Plot point: *"any incident, episode or event that hooks into the action and spins it
around into another direction."* Act I pp.1–30, PP1 ~p.25–27; Act II ~60pp; PP2 ~p.85–90.
*"I cannot emphasize enough that this first ten-page unit of dramatic action is the most
important part of the screenplay."*

**And his own repudiation of how he is taught:**

> "some people have a tendency to want to make a rule for everything… I always give them
> the same answer: '**So what!**' So what if Plot Point I occurs on page 19. **So what!**
> … **The paradigm is only a guide, not an absolute!**"

**Keep this.** Field is the most-caricatured teacher in the field, and the caricature is
what a language model reproduces by default.

### Snyder — the Beat Sheet, verified exactly [P]

110-page assumption. Opening Image 1 · Theme Stated 5 · Set-Up 1–10 · **Catalyst 12** ·
Debate 12–25 · **Break into Two 25** · B Story 30 · Fun and Games 30–55 · **Midpoint 55** ·
Bad Guys Close In 55–75 · **All Is Lost 75** · Dark Night 75–85 · **Break into Three 85** ·
Finale 85–110 · Final Image 110.

**Drift warning:** savethecat.com now publishes *percentages* that don't match the page
numbers (Break into Two p.25 = 22.7%, not 20%). Pick one system and say which.

Named laws worth having: **Pope in the Pool** (bury exposition under a distracting
visual) · **Double Mumbo Jumbo** ("**we are allowed to suspend disbelief once in a
movie**") · **Covenant of the Arc** (every character but the antagonist changes).
**The Board:** 40 cards, four rows; each card carries **+/−** and **`><`** —
*"an emotional change like this must occur in every scene. And if you don't have it, you
don't know what the scene is about."*

### Harmon — the Story Circle [P]

> 1. A character is in a **zone of comfort**, 2. But they **want** something. 3. They
> enter an **unfamiliar situation**, 4. **Adapt** to it, 5. **Get what they wanted**,
> 6. **Pay a heavy price** for it, 7. Then **return** to their familiar situation,
> 8. Having **changed**.

Shorthand: **You · Need · Go · Search · Find · Take · Return · CHANGE** — *"Sounds like a
caveman giving you an order. That's what it is."*

Geometry that matters: **odd numbers are points, even numbers are the arcs between** —
2/4/6/8 are *processes*, not moments. The horizontal line divides Ordinary from Special
World. Opposites pair: 3↔7, 1↔5, **4↔8** — *"the opposite of (8) is (4), the road of
trials. Remember that zippo the bum gave him? It blocked the bullet! … **Why is this not
Deus Ex Machina? Because we earned it (4).**"*

And his calibration pair: a thirsty man gets a soda and returns to the couch — "**that was
'perfect story structure.' On the other hand, the story sucked.**" Versus a car exploding
and a Navy SEAL killing a werewolf — "**very little structure.**" *"Cool stuff with no
structure is like that perfect scene you recorded when you left the lens cap on."*

### Vogler — and his own caveat [P, the 1985 Disney memo]

Twelve stages; eight archetypes (Hero, Mentor, Threshold Guardian, Herald, Shapeshifter,
Shadow, Ally, Trickster). **The archetypes were not in the memo** — added later for the
book.

> "**Following the guidelines of myth too rigidly can lead to a stiff, unnatural
> structure, and there is the danger of being too obvious. The hero myth is a skeleton
> that should be masked with the details of the individual story… the stages can be
> deleted, added to, and drastically re-shuffled without losing any of their power.**"

Archetypes are **functions, not characters** — "a mask a character wears in a particular
scene." Obi-Wan is the Mentor throughout and must wear the Hero's mask to sacrifice
himself.

### Aristotle and Freytag — the substrate [P]

**Aristotle**, *Poetics*: six parts in order of importance — **Plot · Character · Thought ·
Diction · Song · Spectacle**. *"**most important of all is the structure of the
incidents**… there may be [tragedy] without character."* And **spectacle** "**of all the
parts, is the least artistic.**"

Unity of action: *"**Unity of plot does not consist in the Unity of the hero.** … if any
one of [the parts] is displaced or removed, the whole will be disjointed… **a thing whose
presence or absence makes no visible difference is not an organic part of the whole.**"*

**Two structural facts worth flagging:**
1. **Aristotle's macro-division is TWO parts** — *desis* (complication) and *lusis*
   (unravelling), hinged on the reversal. Every midpoint-centred model is closer to
   Aristotle than the three-act model is.
2. **Aristotle never mentions acts.** The "handed down from the Greeks" claim is false;
   the lineage is Renaissance playwrights following Horace, then Lope de Vega.

**Freytag** (1863): his labels are **introduction · rise · climax · return/fall ·
catastrophe** — **not** "exposition" and "dénouement," which are a later flattening.
And the earliest fractal claim in the literature:

> "**Whatever seems separated by a strong incision… must again have an introduction, a
> rise, a proportionate climax, and a conclusion.**"

## 6. Where they genuinely contradict each other

**Never assert one of these as settled.**

| axis | the split |
|---|---|
| **Is the low point at 75% a rule?** | Snyder yes. **Gulino explicitly no** — 3 of 11 case films. Mazin yes, "but not because the books say page 90." |
| **Where is the nadir?** | Snyder/Field/Yorke ~75%. **Vogler and Harmon: at the MIDPOINT.** Unreconciled. |
| **Does the midpoint go up or down?** | Snyder: either. Yorke/Mazin: up. Vogler/Harmon: down. **Gulino: "the actual resolution or its mirror opposite"** — the only one who says why it can be either. |
| **How many acts?** | Field/Snyder 3 · Yorke 5 · Thompson 4 · **Truby: acts are external and wrong** · **Mazin: "There are no acts."** |
| **Is theme a word or a proposition?** | **August: a word is fine** ("mutation," "motherhood"). **Mazin: a word is disqualifying** ("brotherhood… nothing to argue about"). They co-host the same podcast. |
| **Want vs need** | Truby: never conflate. **Mazin: abandons the binary** for "what are they afraid of / how profound is their denial." |
| **Opposition size** | Field/Snyder/Vogler: hero vs antagonist. **Truby: minimum four corners.** |
| **Does the main tension resolve at the end?** | Snyder/Field yes. **Gulino: no — at the end of act two**, measured 70–82%. |

### And the empirical evidence undercuts the three-act model

- **Kristin Thompson**, *Storytelling in the New Hollywood* (100+ films): **four** roughly
  equal parts, each ~20–30 min. *"the 'three-act structure'… fails to explain how
  Hollywood stories are put together."*
- **Cutting & Armstrong (2019)**, human viewers segmenting films: **3 parts ≤90 min,
  4 for 90–150, 5 for ≥150**, each ~20–35 min. Supports Thompson; supports neither the
  rigid three-act nor the eight-sequence unit.
- **Bordwell:** manuals claiming three acts came "from the ancient Greeks" misrepresent
  history.

### Named errors to guard against

1. Several sites publish **Freytag's five parts under Yorke's name**. Yorke's Act 3 is the
   *midpoint*, not the climax.
2. The popular "Sequence A: status quo / B: lock-in / C: raising stakes" naming is **not
   Gulino's** and smuggles in a low point he rejects.
3. **"24 sequences" attributed to Frank Daniel** — no trace anywhere.
4. **Save the Cat's critique**: Peter Suderman, *Slate* — since a page ≈ a minute, BS2 is
   "**essentially a minute-to-minute movie formula**"; when everyone follows it "**movies
   all start to seem the same… like screenplay Mad Libs.**" He wrote the essay to Snyder's
   beats and found it "**made writing easier but also made me less creative.**"

## 7. The causal chain — Parker & Stone

mtvU *Stand In*, NYU, **6 March 2014**.

> **Trey Parker:** "**if the words 'and then' belong between those beats, you're fucked.**
> … **What should happen between every beat is either the word 'therefore' or 'but'.** …
> **Literally we'll sometimes write it out to make sure we're doing it.**"
>
> **Matt Stone:** "…it's those two, '**but**' '**because**', '**therefore**' that gives you
> the causation between each beat, and that's a story."

Stone widens it to **three** connectives, which most retellings drop.

**This is mechanically checkable and it is the single most implementable structural test
in this document:** write the beat list, put a connective between every pair, and any
`and then` is a defect.

## 8. Scene transitions — Gulino's dialogue hook taxonomy

| type | example |
|---|---|
| **dialogue hook** | *Toy Story*: "You'll see. In a few days, everything will be the same." (close-up, ending act one) · *Lawrence*: "Aqaba is over there. It is only a matter of going." |
| **unspoken hook** | *North by Northwest*: the note — "What do I do with him in the morning? —Eve." |
| **visual hook** | *Nights of Cabiria*: a hand indicating seven o'clock; cut to a station clock at 7:00 |
| **appointment / deadline** | telegraphing |
| **plain dangling cause** | a question deliberately unanswered for several scenes |

Three formulations of the same closing beat: **Truby** structural (endpoint of desire =
key word) · **Iglesias** emotional (a hit of visceral emotion) · **McKee** value (the
charge flips).

## 9. "Enter late, leave early" — and the case against it

**Attribution is contested** — Goldman and Mamet about equally, both primary texts
lending-restricted **[restricted]**. Truby's own version is hedged: *"as late as possible
**without losing any of the key structure elements you need.**"*

**Gulino's four scene types the rule would delete** — the strongest counterargument in the
literature, because it is a taxonomy rather than an opinion:

| type | verbatim |
|---|---|
| **Preparation** | "designed to create an expectation — usually hope or fear — paid off either directly or **by contrast**… **can usually be cut out entirely without affecting the plot, but they greatly enhance the emotional impact.**" |
| **Retardation** | "some promised event is presented to the audience, **which is then made to wait for it, the effect being the buildup of anticipation.**" |
| **Aftermath** | "**follow emotionally charged scenes… little or no dialogue or activity… heavily atmospheric**… can usually be cut without affecting the plot, but they have a profound impact." |
| **Recapitulation** | "characters recount briefly where the story has been… **thus it serves both to review and create anticipation.**" |

> "**Storytellers who fail to consider these issues run the risk of writing a script that
> leaves the reader lost, disengaged, and disenchanted.**"

**The reconciliation:** "enter late, leave early" is a rule about **plot economy** being
misapplied as a rule about **emotional economy**. Cut the arrival and the greeting; do not
cut the aftermath.

## 10. One constraint specific to a video pipeline

**Aristotle ranks spectacle last of six and calls it "the least artistic."** Every
canonical source puts plot first and image last. For a pipeline whose output feeds an AI
video model, that ordering is worth encoding as a constraint rather than a footnote — the
temptation runs entirely the other way.
